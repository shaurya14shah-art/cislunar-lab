Earth–Moon Playground

Fly a spacecraft between the Earth and the Moon. Four short experiments, in order, each one showing an idea your research paper depends on. Every path you see is calculated live from Newton's law of gravity.

  1. The Moon goes round the Earth once a month. If you turn your head at the same rate, the Moon appears to stand still. That is the trick the whole subject uses: it turns a moving target into a fixed map.

    Switch between the two while the spacecraft flies. Its path is a simple loop from outside and a flower pattern when you turn with the Moon. It is one flight, drawn two ways.

    The price of turning: two extra pushes appear, just as on a merry-go-round. One throws things outward (centrifugal). One pushes moving things sideways (Coriolis). The other three experiments use the turning view.

  2. There are exactly five places where Earth's pull, the Moon's pull and the outward push cancel. A spacecraft placed there, at rest, stays. They are called L1 to L5. The question is what happens if you miss by a little.

    Choose a point.

    Try L1 with a 10 km miss, then L4 with the same miss. One runs away, the other circles forever.

    L1, L2 and L3 are like a ball balanced on a hilltop: any miss grows, and it grows by the same factor in each equal stretch of time (exponential growth). L4 and L5 are like a ball in a bowl. Spacecraft still use L1 and L2 because a small, regular correction is enough to stay.

  3. A thrown ball cannot rise higher than its energy allows. The same is true here: for a given energy there are regions the spacecraft can never enter. On the chart they are drawn as land; open water is where it may go.

    Start with low energy: the Moon is sealed in its own lake. Raise the energy until a channel opens at L1, then until one opens at L2. Launch each time and see where the spacecraft can get to.

    The quantity that stays constant is called the Jacobi constant, C. Confusingly, a larger C means less energy, which is why this slider is labelled by energy instead. The coastline is where the spacecraft would have zero speed.

  4. The dark loop near L2 is a parking orbit. It is unstable, like the hilltop. But there is a set of paths that glide onto it with no engine burn at all (green), and a set that leave it (orange). Your paper asks: if you start on a green path with a small error, do you still arrive?

    Launch at 1 m, then 100 m, 300 m, 1 km and 10 km. Each launch adds a dot to the small graph. You are rebuilding Figure 4 of your paper by hand.

    A spacecraft counts as arrived if it stays within 5,000 km of the loop for one full lap, with no corrections. The loop here is the flat cousin of the halo orbits in your paper, and this is one route out of many. For this route, about half arrive at an error near 360 m.
